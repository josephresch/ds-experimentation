**The data is real and runs.** `data/writers.csv`, `data/requests.csv`, `data/answers.csv`, generated by
`data/make_data.R` with a fixed seed, so the script reproduces the files exactly. Everything is synthetic.
There is no public Quora experiment dataset in R, and the Quora data that is public is the Question Pairs
duplicate-detection corpus, which is an NLP dataset and useless for this. So the data was built to carry
the specific problems a real writer-side experiment has.

**Four things are planted in the data.** Do not read this list before practicing.

<details>
<summary>Planted problems (spoiler)</summary>

1. 40 writers appear twice in `writers.csv` with opposite arms, and they are disproportionately the most

3. `answers.csv` is the platform-wide table. 3,400 rows belong to writers outside the experiment and 900

4. The outcome is heavy-tailed and 70% zero, which decides what test is appropriate.

Problem 2 changes the answer. Problem 1 does not change the result much and is still the most important
thing to report, for reasons in Task 1.

## Task 0, the brief

About 4 minutes.

**Interviewer.** "You're picking this up from someone who left. The experiment has finished and nobody
has analyzed it. Here's the brief and three files."

> ### Brief
>
> **The product.** When a question is posted, Quora routes answer requests to writers who look likely to
> answer it. Writers see them as notifications and on an Answer Requests page. Answer volume has been
> drifting down for a while and this is the main lever the team has on it.
>
> **The change.** A new routing model. The thesis is that the old one sent too many poorly matched
> requests, which trained writers to ignore them. The new one sends **fewer requests to better-matched
> writers**. Fewer requests is a design choice, not a side effect.
>
> **The experiment.** Writers eligible for answer requests were randomized 50/50, stratified on prior
> answering activity. It ran 14 days, 2026-08-18 to 2026-08-31 inclusive.
>
> **What the team wants.** Ship it or not, and why.
>
> ### Files
>
> `writers.csv`
>
> | Column | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | writer_id | Writer |
> | arm | control or treatment |
> | signup_date | When they joined Quora |
> | prior_answers_90d | Answers written in the 90 days before the experiment |
> | topics_followed | Topics followed at experiment start |
>
> `requests.csv`
>
> | Column | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | request_id | The request |
> | writer_id | Who it went to |
> | question_id | What they were asked |
> | sent_ts | When it was sent |
>
> `answers.csv`
>
> | Column | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | answer_id | The answer |
> | writer_id | Who wrote it |
> | question_id | What it answers |
> | written_ts | When |
> | upvotes_7d | Upvotes in its first 7 days |
> | from_request | 1 if written in response to a request, 0 if found another way |

**Interviewer.** "Before you open anything. What are you going to compute, and what would make you say no?"

**Listen for.**

- The outcome named as **answers written per writer**, and the reason: the business wants more answers,
  and the writer is the randomization unit so it is the unit the outcome has to be measured on.
- **The trap identified before it is seen.** The treatment cuts requests by design. So any metric with
  requests in the denominator will improve whether or not a single extra answer got written. Saying this
  in Task 0, before opening the files, is the strongest possible start and costs nothing.
- A guardrail named: answer quality, available as upvotes.
- A decision rule, even a loose one: more total answers without quality falling.

**Traps.** Opening the files before saying what he is looking for. Naming answers per request as the
primary. No guardrail.

**Score.** 4 starts coding. 6 names a per-writer outcome and a guardrail. 8 also calls the ratio trap
before seeing the data.

## Task 1, load, grain, validity

About 13 minutes.

**Interviewer.** "Go ahead and load them. Tell me what you're looking at."

**Listen for.**

- **Grain stated for each table, out loud.** One row per writer, one row per request, one row per answer.
  Then verified rather than assumed, because two of the three are wrong.
- Checking uniqueness of the key in each table. That is what finds problems 1 and 2.
- Checking that `answers.csv` is scoped to the experiment. It is not.
- Checking the date range against the stated window. 900 rows fail it.
- A sample ratio mismatch check on the arms after cleaning.

**What is there to find.**

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| `writers.csv` rows against unique `writer_id` | 24,040 against 24,000. 40 writers appear twice, in opposite arms |
| Those 40 writers' `prior_answers_90d` | Median 29.5, against 0 for everyone else. The bug landed on the most active writers |
| `requests.csv` rows against unique `request_id` | 69,191 against 66,105. 3,086 duplicate rows |
| Duplicates by arm | **2,894 treatment, 192 control.** Not symmetric |
| `answers.csv` writers not in `writers.csv` | 3,400 rows |
| `answers.csv` rows before 2026-08-18 | 900 rows |
| Arm split after cleaning | 11,977 against 11,983, chi-square p = 0.97. No mismatch |

**Strong signals.**

- **Noticing the duplicate requests are concentrated in treatment, and stopping to think about it.** This
  is the highest-value observation in the round. Symmetric duplicates are noise. Duplicates that sit
  almost entirely in the treatment arm are produced by the thing being tested, which means the new
  routing service has a retry bug, which means the request counts are wrong in exactly the arm whose
  request count is the whole point.
- **Treating the 40 dual-assigned writers as a validity finding, not just a cleaning step.** Dropping them
  changes the headline by about 0.2 points, so on the numbers it hardly matters. It matters because
  assignment was supposed to happen once per writer and did not, the failures are concentrated in the
  writers who produce most of the output, and the same bug is presumably live in every other experiment
  on this surface. Reporting a bug whose effect here is small, because its effect elsewhere may not be,
  is the judgment being tested.
- Saying what he is *not* checking and why. There is no time to validate everything in 60 minutes.

**Follow-ups.**

| Follow-up | Answer |
|---|---|
| "You dropped 40 writers out of 24,000. Does that matter?" | Not for this result, it moves the headline by about a fifth of a point. It matters because it tells me assignment can fire twice for the same writer, it fired on the most active ones, and nothing here was designed to catch that. I'd report it whatever this experiment concludes |
| "Why not just keep the first row for each of those writers?" | Because I don't know which arm they actually experienced, and they're heavy writers, so whichever I pick I'm assigning high-volume outcomes to an arm on a coin flip. Forty writers of noise is cheaper than forty writers of possibly-wrong signal |
| "The duplicate requests are in one arm. So what?" | It means the duplication is caused by the treatment, so it isn't a logging nuisance, it's a property of the new service. Practically, it inflates treatment's request count, so without deduping I'd understate how much the new routing actually cut requests, which is the mechanism I'm trying to measure |
| "Do you need the pre-experiment answers?" | Not for the outcome, I'd filter them out. They'd be useful as a pre-period covariate if I wanted to tighten the estimate later |

**Reference.**

```r
w <- read.csv("writers.csv",  stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
r <- read.csv("requests.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
a <- read.csv("answers.csv",  stringsAsFactors = FALSE)

c(writer_rows  = nrow(w), unique_writers  = length(unique(w$writer_id)),
  request_rows = nrow(r), unique_requests = length(unique(r$request_id)),
  answer_rows  = nrow(a), unique_answers  = length(unique(a$answer_id)))

dual <- names(which(table(w$writer_id) > 1))
summary(w$prior_answers_90d[w$writer_id %in% dual])   # the bug hit active writers

# are the duplicate requests symmetric across arms? they are not
tapply(duplicated(r$request_id), w$arm[match(r$writer_id, w$writer_id)], sum)

START <- as.POSIXct("2026-08-18", tz = "UTC"); END <- as.POSIXct("2026-09-01", tz = "UTC")
a$ts <- as.POSIXct(a$written_ts, tz = "UTC")
c(before_start = sum(a$ts < START), not_in_experiment = sum(!a$writer_id %in% w$writer_id))

w2 <- w[!w$writer_id %in% dual, ]
r2 <- r[!duplicated(r$request_id) & r$writer_id %in% w2$writer_id, ]
a2 <- a[a$ts >= START & a$ts < END & a$writer_id %in% w2$writer_id, ]

table(w2$arm); chisq.test(table(w2$arm))$p.value       # 11977 / 11983, p = 0.97
```

**Traps.**

- Assuming one row per writer and joining on it. `merge(a, w)` adds 332 rows silently.
- Deduping the requests without looking at which arm they are in.
- Computing on `answers.csv` without scoping it to the experiment and the window.
- Spending 25 minutes here. The cleaning is five lines once you know what to look for, and the round has
  to reach an outcome.

**Score.** 4 loads and starts computing. 6 checks keys, scopes the tables, and runs a sample ratio check.
8 also spots that the request duplication sits in one arm and says what that implies about the service,
and treats the dual assignment as a bug worth reporting regardless of its effect here.

## Task 2, the metric and the first read

About 14 minutes.

**Interviewer.** "Build me the numbers."

**What the data gives.**

| Metric, per writer over 14 days | Control | Treatment | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Requests received | 3.397 | 2.066 | -39.2% |
| Answers written | 1.112 | 1.038 | -6.7% |
| Total answers written | 13,316 | 12,433 | -883 answers |
| Answers per request sent | 0.327 | 0.502 | **+53.5%** |

**Interviewer, once he has the ratio.** "Answers per request is up 53%. The routing team is going to lead
with that. Are they right?"

**Listen for.**

- **No.** The treatment cut requests 39% by design, so the denominator is a treated quantity. Dividing by
  it converts a deliberate reduction into an apparent improvement. The ratio would rise if the new model
  sent one request and got one answer, which would be a catastrophe for answer volume.
- The decision-relevant number is **total answers**, because that is what the business gets. It is down
  883 over 14 days.
- Per-writer answers as the test statistic, because writers were the randomization unit.
- Noticing the ratio is not useless, it is just not the outcome. It says the matching genuinely improved,
  which is the treatment's thesis and is worth knowing, because it tells you the fix is to keep the
  matching and stop cutting volume.

**Follow-ups.**

| Follow-up | Answer |
|---|---|
| "So the new model is worse at matching?" | The opposite, it's clearly better at matching. Answer rate per request went up 53% and that part is real. The problem is that it also cut request volume 39%, and the volume loss is bigger than the matching gain. Those are two separate decisions that got shipped as one |
| "Should we report the ratio at all?" | Yes, as a mechanism, labeled as one. It is the evidence that the matching model works. It is not evidence that we got more answers, and I'd put it below the total in any writeup so nobody reads it as the headline |
| "What if I told you the routing team's KR is answers per request?" | Then their KR rewards sending fewer requests, and this experiment is what that looks like when it works. That's worth raising separately from this launch decision |
| "Why per writer and not per request?" | Because writers were randomized, not requests. A per-request average compares different numbers of requests between arms and the units aren't independent within a writer anyway |

**Reference.**

```r
w2$n_req <- as.integer(table(factor(r2$writer_id, levels = w2$writer_id)))
w2$n_ans <- as.integer(table(factor(a2$writer_id, levels = w2$writer_id)))

agg <- function(x, g) c(control = mean(x[g == "control"]), treatment = mean(x[g == "treatment"]))
rbind(requests = agg(w2$n_req, w2$arm), answers = agg(w2$n_ans, w2$arm))

tot <- function(x, g) c(sum(x[g == "control"]), sum(x[g == "treatment"]))
tot(w2$n_ans, w2$arm)                                   # 13316 vs 12433
(tot(w2$n_ans, w2$arm) / tot(w2$n_req, w2$arm))         # 0.327 vs 0.502, +53.5%
```

**Traps.** Leading with answers per request. Reporting only rates and never the total. Computing the
answer count from `answers.csv` without the Task 1 filters.

**Score.** 4 reports the ratio as the result. 6 uses per-writer answers and names the denominator problem.
8 also separates the two things the treatment did, and says the ratio is real evidence about matching
while being the wrong outcome.

## Task 3, choosing the test

About 11 minutes.

**Interviewer.** "Put an interval on the answers number."

**What the data gives.**

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect on answers per writer | -0.074, or -6.7% |
| 95% CI | -16.3% to +2.9% |
| p | 0.17 |
| Distribution | mean 1.07, median 0, SD 4.21, max 138, 70% zeros |
| Top 1% of writers | 31% of all answers |
| Wilcoxon rank-sum p | 0.65 |
| Adjusting for `prior_answers_90d` | -8.1%, CI -16.4% to +0.3%, p = 0.057 |

**Listen for.**

- **A Welch t-test on the mean, defended.** The reflex answer, that the data is skewed and 70% zeros so a
  t-test is invalid, is wrong and this round is a good place to get it wrong. The t-test does not assume
  the data is normal. It assumes the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal, and with
  roughly 12,000 per arm the central limit theorem covers a tail like this one. A candidate who reaches
  for a rank test because the histogram looks bad is applying a rule he has not thought about.
- **Why a rank test is the wrong tool here even though it runs.** Wilcoxon tests whether one arm's draws
  tend to exceed the other's. The business question is whether Quora gets more answers in total, which is
  a question about the mean, because total equals mean times writers. With 70% ties at zero the rank test
  is answering a question nobody asked, and it says p = 0.65 while the quantity we care about is down 7%.
- **Reading the interval for harm, not just for significance.** p = 0.17 is not evidence of no effect. The
  interval runs to -16.3%, so a large loss has not been ruled out. Saying "not significant, so no harm"
  is the single worst available answer and it is the exact failure this case is built to catch.
- **The sign.** R orders factor levels alphabetically, so `t.test(n_ans ~ arm)` returns control minus
  treatment. The printed interval is +0.032 to -0.181 in that direction. Reading it as the treatment
  effect without flipping it reverses the recommendation.

**Strong signals.**

- Offering regression adjustment on `prior_answers_90d` as a way to buy precision, and being honest that
  it buys about 13% off the standard error rather than rescuing the design. Prior activity is
  pre-treatment, so adjusting on it is legitimate.
- Saying what the experiment could have detected. With this variance and this n, the smallest detectable
  effect is around 14% to 15%, so the design was never able to resolve a 7% change. That reframes the
  result from "no effect" to "the test could not answer the question at this sample size."
- Bootstrapping the mean as a check on the t-interval, and finding it agrees, and saying so briefly
  instead of treating it as a separate finding.

**Follow-ups.**

| Follow-up | Answer |
|---|---|
| "It's not significant. So it's safe to ship?" | No. Not significant here means the test couldn't tell, not that nothing happened. The interval goes to -16%, which on answer supply is a serious loss, and the design was only ever powered for about 14%. The honest statement is that this experiment cannot rule out real harm |
| "70% of the values are zero. Doesn't that break the t-test?" | It breaks normality of the data, which the t-test doesn't assume. What it needs is the sampling distribution of the mean to be roughly normal, and at 12,000 per arm it is, even with this tail. I'd bootstrap as a sanity check and I'd expect it to agree |
| "Then why not use Wilcoxon, it's more robust?" | It's robust to a different question. It asks whether treatment draws tend to exceed control draws, and we want total answers, which is a mean. With this many ties at zero it's also doing something strange. Robustness that changes the estimand isn't robustness |
| "Would a Poisson or negative binomial model be better?" | A negative binomial fits counts like this better and would give a tighter interval, and I'd use it if the interval were the deciding factor. It doesn't change the story and it adds assumptions I'd have to defend, so I'd run it after I had the simple answer, not instead of it |

**Reference.**

```r
c(mean = mean(w2$n_ans), median = median(w2$n_ans), sd = sd(w2$n_ans),
  max = max(w2$n_ans), pct_zero = mean(w2$n_ans == 0))

tt <- t.test(n_ans ~ arm, data = w2)      # NOTE: control - treatment, alphabetical
ctl <- mean(w2$n_ans[w2$arm == "control"])
c(effect_pct = (mean(w2$n_ans[w2$arm == "treatment"]) / ctl - 1) * 100,
  lo = -tt$conf.int[2] / ctl * 100, hi = -tt$conf.int[1] / ctl * 100, p = tt$p.value)

wilcox.test(n_ans ~ arm, data = w2)$p.value              # 0.65, different hypothesis

summary(lm(n_ans ~ arm + prior_answers_90d, data = w2))$coefficients["armtreatment", ]
```

**Traps.** Abandoning the t-test because the data is skewed. Reporting Wilcoxon as the headline. Calling
0.17 "no effect." Getting the sign backwards. Fitting a zero-inflated model before reporting a mean.

**Score.** 4 reads p = 0.17 as no effect, or switches to a rank test on sight of the histogram. 6 uses the
right test and reports the interval. 8 defends the t-test on the CLT rather than on the data's shape,
reads the interval for the harm it cannot rule out, and says what the design was powered to detect.

## Task 4, where the effect actually is

About 13 minutes.

**Interviewer.** "The overall number can't tell us much. Can you get anything more out of this?"

**What the data gives.** Segments on `prior_answers_90d`, a pre-treatment variable.

| Segment | Writers | Control answers/writer | Change | 95% CI | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dormant, 0 | 13,199 | 0.048 | -3.2% | -18.7% to +12.3% | 0.69 |
| Light, 1 to 4 | 6,137 | 0.616 | +1.4% | -5.8% to +8.5% | 0.71 |
| Mid, 5 to 19 | 3,506 | 2.124 | **+8.9%** | +2.6% to +15.3% | 0.006 |
| Heavy, 20+ | 1,118 | 13.279 | **-17.6%** | -30.7% to -4.4% | 0.009 |

Share of all answers written, control arm: dormant 2.4%, light 14.2%, mid 28.0%, **heavy 55.4%**.

Quality guardrail, at the answer grain: upvotes per answer, difference not distinguishable from zero,
CI -2.8% to +4.3%.

**Listen for.**

- **Segmenting on a pre-treatment covariate, and saying that is why it is allowed.** `prior_answers_90d`
  was fixed before assignment, so cutting on it cannot be contaminated by the treatment. Cutting on
  something the treatment moved, such as requests received, would be. A candidate who segments without
  making this distinction gets partial credit; one who segments on requests received has made a real error.
- **The finding.** The new routing helps mid-tier writers and hurts heavy writers, and heavy writers
  produce 55% of all answers. That is why the overall number is negative and why it is hard to detect:
  two real effects of opposite sign, with the negative one attached to most of the volume.
- **The mechanism, stated plainly.** Heavy writers were already well matched, so better matching bought
  them little, and cutting their request volume took away answers they would have written. Mid-tier
  writers were the ones getting poorly matched requests, so they gained.
- The guardrail checked and reported as flat, with the note that flat quality alongside fewer answers is
  not reassurance. It means we lost answers without gaining anything on quality.
- Why segmenting finds what the overall test could not: 70% of writers write nothing, so they contribute
  variance and no signal. Concentrating on the writers who can move rescues the power.

**Follow-ups.**

| Follow-up | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Four segments, four tests. Aren't you fishing?" | The cuts are on a pre-treatment variable, they're the obvious ones for a writer experiment, and I'd have named them before looking. If you want the correction, mid survives Bonferroni at four tests comfortably and heavy is borderline. What convinces me isn't the p-values, it's that the two significant segments point in opposite directions with a mechanism that explains both |
| "Could you segment on how many requests they received?" | No. Requests received is exactly what the treatment changed, so the segments wouldn't be comparable between arms. Prior activity is safe because it was fixed before assignment |
| "Quality is flat. Isn't that good?" | It's the absence of a second problem, not a benefit. We're writing fewer answers at the same quality, which is just fewer answers |
| "Which segment would you have guessed before looking?" | I'd have expected heavy writers to lose, because they had the least to gain from better matching and the most to lose from fewer requests. I wouldn't have predicted the size |

**Reference.**

```r
w2$seg <- cut(w2$prior_answers_90d, c(-1, 0, 4, 19, Inf),
              labels = c("dormant", "light", "mid", "heavy"))   # pre-treatment

do.call(rbind, lapply(levels(w2$seg), function(s) {
  d <- w2[w2$seg == s, ]; t <- t.test(n_ans ~ arm, data = d)
  m <- mean(d$n_ans[d$arm == "control"])
  data.frame(segment = s, n = nrow(d), control = round(m, 3),
             pct = round((mean(d$n_ans[d$arm == "treatment"]) / m - 1) * 100, 1),
             lo = round(-t$conf.int[2] / m * 100, 1), hi = round(-t$conf.int[1] / m * 100, 1),
             p  = signif(t$p.value, 2))
}))

round(tapply(w2$n_ans[w2$arm == "control"], w2$seg[w2$arm == "control"], sum) /
      sum(w2$n_ans[w2$arm == "control"]), 3)            # heavy = 55.4% of supply

a2$arm <- w2$arm[match(a2$writer_id, w2$writer_id)]
t.test(upvotes_7d ~ arm, data = a2)$conf.int            # quality guardrail, flat
```

**Traps.** Not segmenting at all. Segmenting on a post-treatment variable. Reporting four p-values with no
mechanism. Missing that heavy writers carry most of the volume, which is what makes their loss decisive.

**Score.** 4 stops at the overall number. 6 segments on prior activity and finds the split. 8 says why a
pre-treatment cut is legitimate, ties the two effects to a mechanism, and weights the segments by the
answer supply they carry.

## Task 5, recommendation

About 5 minutes.

**Interviewer.** "Two minutes. What do you tell the team?"

**Model answer.** "Don't ship it as it stands. The number the routing team will lead with, answers per
request up 53%, is an artifact of the treatment cutting requests 39% on purpose, so it rewards the
denominator moving. On the number that matters, we wrote 883 fewer answers in 14 days, down about 7% per
writer, and that estimate is not significant but the interval runs to -16%, so this test cannot rule out
serious harm. It was only powered for about 14%. The useful finding is underneath: mid-tier writers wrote
9% more and heavy writers wrote 18% less, and heavy writers produce 55% of all answers, which is both why
the total is negative and why the overall test couldn't see it. The mechanism fits, since heavy writers
were already well matched and the new model mostly just sent them less work. Quality was flat throughout.
So the matching model is good and should be kept, and the volume cut is what is costing us. I'd re-run
with the new matching applied at the old request volume for the heavy segment and see whether the mid-tier
gain survives without the heavy-tier loss. Two things I'd report separately from the launch decision: the
new routing service is writing duplicate request rows, almost entirely in its own arm, and 40 writers got
assigned to both arms, concentrated among the most active ones, which is an assignment bug that will be
affecting other experiments on this surface too."

**What pushes it to an 8.**

- Separating the two things the treatment did, and recommending keeping one and reversing the other. The
  data supports it and it is the only recommendation that uses the whole analysis.
- Reporting the two bugs as findings in their own right, not as cleaning steps.
- Naming the power problem as the reason the headline is quiet, rather than letting quiet read as safe.

**Traps.** Leading with the method. Recommending a re-run with no design change. "Inconclusive" with no
verdict. Forgetting the bugs. Recommending ship because nothing was significant.

**Score.** 4 gives no verdict or ships on non-significance. 6 says don't ship and cites the total and the
segments. 8 splits matching from volume, proposes the specific re-run, and escalates both bugs.
